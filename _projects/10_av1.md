## Why it is needed

Streaming 8K 360-degree video by sending the whole sphere wastes most of the bandwidth. The viewer only sees a small part of it at any moment. The alternative is to split the frame into tiles and send only the tiles in view. That works only if each tile can be decoded on its own.

Normal encoders break this. Motion estimation will happily reference pixels outside the tile, and the in-loop filters run across tile boundaries. A tile decoded without its neighbours then drifts and shows visible seams. The limit has to be enforced inside the encoder, not patched afterwards.

## What I did

I implemented motion-constrained encoding in the AV1 encoder so tiles stay independently decodable:

- **Motion-vector search-range limits**, so motion estimation cannot reference pixels outside the tile
- **Loop-filter limits** across all three AV1 in-loop stages: deblocking, CDEF and loop restoration. Each one would otherwise pull in data from a neighbouring tile.
- Several encoder speed-ups, because the added limits cost search time that had to be won back somewhere

AV1's three-stage in-loop filter is what makes this harder than the same problem in HEVC. If you constrain one stage and leave another alone, information still crosses the boundary. The artefact only appears when a tile is decoded on its own, so normal encoding tests will not show it.

## Result

- Published at ICTC 2019: _Advanced Motion-Constrained AV1 Encoder for 8K 360 VR Tiled Streaming_
- Granted US patent **US11290714B2**, _Motion-constrained AV1 encoding method and apparatus for tiled streaming_

This sits alongside a longer background in video compression: H.264/AVC, HEVC, MPEG-DASH adaptive streaming, and a tiled H.264 encoder ported into FFmpeg. That is where the habits behind my later edge work came from. Codec engineering is a strict school for thinking about compute budgets.
