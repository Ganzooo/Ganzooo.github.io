## Why it is needed

Streaming 8K 360-degree video by sending the whole sphere is wasteful: the viewer sees a fraction of it at any moment. The alternative is to split the frame into tiles and send only the tiles in view — but that only works if each tile can be decoded on its own.

Ordinary encoders break this. Motion estimation happily references pixels outside the tile, and in-loop filters run across tile boundaries, so a tile decoded without its neighbours produces drift and visible seams. The constraint has to be enforced inside the encoder, not patched afterwards.

## What I did

Implemented motion-constrained encoding in the AV1 encoder so that tiles remain independently decodable:

- **Motion-vector search-range restriction** so motion estimation cannot reference pixels outside the tile
- **Loop-filter restriction** across all three AV1 in-loop stages — deblocking, CDEF and loop restoration — each of which would otherwise pull in neighbouring tile data
- Several encoder speed-up algorithms, since the added constraints cost search time that had to be won back elsewhere

AV1's three-stage in-loop filter is what makes this harder than the equivalent problem in HEVC: constraining one stage and leaving another unconstrained still leaks information across the boundary, and the resulting artefact only appears when a tile is decoded in isolation — not during normal encoding tests.

## Result

- Published at **ICTC 2019**: _Advanced Motion-Constrained AV1 Encoder for 8K 360 VR Tiled Streaming_
- Granted US patent **US11290714B2** — _Motion-constrained AV1 encoding method and apparatus for tiled streaming_

This work sits alongside a longer background in video compression — H.264/AVC, HEVC, MPEG-DASH adaptive streaming, and a tiled H.264 encoder ported into FFmpeg — which is where the edge-deployment instincts in my later computer-vision work came from. Codec engineering is an unusually strict school for thinking about compute budgets.
